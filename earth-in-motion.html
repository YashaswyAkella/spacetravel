Read the science & model notes Reference frames, scales, assumptions, and sources

What the animation means

There is no universal, stationary grid defining “the same point in space.” Earth returns near the same position relative to the Sun after a year while the Solar System has advanced relative to the Milky Way. The Sun and planets share Galactic motion; gravity holds the planetary system together.

Planet's Galactic position = Sun's Galactic position + planet's position relative to the Sun

The first view records the combined trajectory, then moves the camera with today's model Sun. The second subtracts the Sun's position separately at every past instant. Changing the trail's reference frame changes its shape.

What is physical, and what is simplified

  • Planetary motion: fixed Kepler ellipses use JPL's approximate J2000 shapes, orientations, and sidereal periods. The Earth–Moon barycentre represents Earth. Starting phases are illustrative; this is not a current sky map or an ephemeris. Planet–planet perturbations, the Sun's barycentric wobble, and secular evolution are omitted.
  • Illustrative scale: a planet's orbital coordinates are uniformly rescaled so its displayed semimajor axis is √a, with a measured in AU. Earth's orbit keeps its reference size. Solar drift is 2 display units per year instead of 48.518 AU per year. All eight planets retain one shared clock and their physical period ratios.
  • Physical scale: orbital distances and solar displacement use the same AU scale. Planet and Sun markers remain enlarged. Long histories show at most 50 turns per planet to prevent unreadable, aliased inner-planet trails.
  • Local Galactic motion: a straight path at 230 km/s approximates a tiny portion of the Galactic orbit. The Sun's small peculiar velocity and long-term Galactic curvature are omitted in the years-scale views. The local grey reference plane passes through the Sun and is parallel to the Galactic midplane; it is not the disc itself.
  • Orientation: the ecliptic is inclined about 60.2° to the Galactic plane. The coordinate rotation also specifies its direction in that plane. Its normal is about 30.4° from the idealized travel direction, so these are oblique, helix-like tracks, not perfect circular springs. Earth's axial tilt is a different angle.
  • Galaxy overview: a separate million-year clock drives an illustrative 230-million-year circle and an 80-million-year full vertical oscillation. Model time 0 is today; rings mark where the model Sun was when the first dinosaurs appeared (≈230 Myr ago) and when they died out (66 Myr ago). The chosen vertical amplitude is 80 parsecs before the 15× display exaggeration. Spiral arms are decorative, not an observed map; their evolution is omitted. The trajectory is illustrative, not a reconstruction of the Sun's past or future.

A clarification about “30 million years”

A complete up-and-down oscillation and the interval between Galactic midplane crossings are different: there are two crossings per cycle. Estimates depend on the Galactic model. For example, older work discusses a roughly 33-million-year half-period, while more recent orbit modelling shows that the Sun’s distant past and future trajectory cannot be determined precisely. This explorer uses 80 million years per full cycle purely to illustrate the geometry.

Sagittarius A* and the galactic centre

Sagittarius A* is a black hole of about 4.3 million solar masses, roughly 26,000 light-years away. In the first two views it marks the real direction of the galactic centre (the “to Sgr A*” axis on the compass). The Sun’s 230-million-year orbit is governed by the combined mass of the inner galaxy—stars, gas and dark matter. Sgr A* marks the centre, but at our distance it supplies only a tiny fraction of that pull.

The fourth view uses approximate published orbits for the stars S2, S38 and S55, assuming a distance of 8.28 kpc. Speeds come from the vis-viva equation. Sizes are enlarged: the real event horizon is only about 0.17 AU across. Orientation is approximate, and relativistic precession is omitted.

Controls

Drag with a mouse or one finger to rotate; scroll, pinch, or use +/− to zoom. With the canvas focused, use arrow keys to rotate, +/− to zoom, and R to reset the view. Space pauses or resumes outside form controls. The timeline pauses playback when moved. Selecting “+1 year” advances the shared local clock; the galaxy view uses “+10 Myr.” Reduced-motion preferences start the animation paused.
